Theorizing the digital: A call to Action for the Archaeological community 
- what are the gaps in our capacities to write contemporary and future archaeological theory as digital archeologists
- Gaps include: ubiquity of computational approaches, using technology despite not knowing the philosophical or cultural dimensions of the digital tools and technologies we employ in this discipline. Lack of code of ethics. 
     - do we actually know the history of the data collection tools we use? We need to understand to adequately communicate the history and impact of the tool in our data collection. not looking into their socio-technical dimensions
- Issue with visibility and impact of 'digital archaeology' as a discipline - rarely citing them as meaningful players in disciplinary philosophizing - do not have the building blocks in place to impact these larger conceptual shifts in the discipline.

- Personally - I never really considered the social dimension/impact of tools beyond their history and roots in military. after class and the readings, I am called to add more reflexive theory in my archaeological practice in the digital age. Like any discipline should practice, reflexive theory in digital archaeology should include the scope of research - specifically regarding the tools we use. like mentioned in the text by Perry and Taylor: "However, the foundations for a critical digital archaeology are being laid, and by our reckoning, digital practitioners now have a responsibility to recognize and actively shape their proportions. In order to suggest a framework that can be mapped out to ensure the future technological developments in the discipline are always and necessarily subjected to consideration of their socio-politial-economic dimensions"
-   - in essense, we need to be reflexive about the technological developments in the discipline. The article suggests systems that can literally force digital archaeologists to be reflexive. These systems include literally developing workflows that are purposefully slow to create time for reflexivity. I agree with this, we aren't really saving lives here in archaeology, none of the work we do really needs to be done quickly. So why don't we work on forcing a slow down, to allow for greater reflexivity. I can already hear all the rebuttals about this, time is money, blah blah blah, but the saying measure twice & cut once is something I think applies a bit here.. In the archaeological discipline as a whole, we are trying to slow down our invasive excavations in order to preserve parts of sites for future excavations that may have less invasive technology. Pausing before drawing conclusions should be inherent in this discipline, in order to embrace the complexities of the data rather than generalizing.
